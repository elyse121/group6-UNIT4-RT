# Mini Project 4.2: Multi-Rate Environmental Monitoring Pipeline

## 1. What this project does

One small gateway reads several environmental sensors. Each sensor needs its own sampling period, but the gateway has a single processing loop and can read only one sensor at a time. The project builds this gateway in simulation, adds a scheduling rule that decides who is read first, and reports how well each sensor's timing was respected.

| Stream | Required period | Normal read time | Alert threshold |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vibration (fast, load 2 only) | 50 ms | 3-6 ms | above 0.8 g |
| Temperature | 100 ms | 4-8 ms | above 28.5 degC |
| Humidity | 240 ms | 6-12 ms | above 68 %RH |
| Air quality | 500 ms | 15-30 ms | above 90 ug/m3 |

The periods differ by more than 2 times, as the lab requires. The signals are synthetic: slow drifts for temperature and humidity, and short spikes for air quality and vibration. Every read has a **long-tail processing time**: with probability 8% it takes 40-160 ms instead of the normal few milliseconds. That is the overrun.

## 2. How the simulation works

- The gateway loop is **non-preemptive**: once a read starts, nothing interrupts it. The simulated run is 60 s.
- When the loop is free, a stream is **due** if its latest release has not been read yet. A scheduling rule picks one due stream.
- If a stream's release is never read before its next release, that sample is **missed** (a fresh reading is more useful than a stale one).
- A sample is **on time** if its read starts within 20% of the period after the due time, **late** if it starts later than that, and **missed** if it never starts.
- **Overrun** of a stream is how much a gap between two consecutive reads exceeds the required period (mean and maximum are reported).
- **Alerts:** the true signal is checked at every instant to find when it really crosses the threshold. The gateway raises an alert at the end of the first read that finds the signal above the threshold. Both times are logged (`results/alert_log_seed1.csv`).
- All rules face exactly the same random read times in each run, so differences come only from the rule. Tables of "30 runs" average 30 different random workloads.

### Scheduling rules compared

- **FP, fixed priority:** the due stream with the shortest period always goes first (rate-monotonic order).
- **EDF, earliest deadline first:** the due stream whose next release (its deadline) is soonest goes first.
- **RR, round-robin:** the due streams take turns in a fixed circle, ignoring urgency.

FP and EDF are the two rules requested. RR is added as a baseline that ignores timing.

### Loads compared

- **3 streams:** temperature, humidity, air quality. The gateway is busy about 26% of the time.
- **4 streams:** the same three plus the fast 50 ms vibration sensor. The gateway is busy about 47% of the time.

## Required report

**Which stream suffered most?** The fast streams, and which one is worst depends on the rule. With 3 streams under FP or EDF, every stream is on time about 94% of the time, but temperature (100 ms period) has the most misses (0.5%, against 0.1% and 0.0%). After adding the 50 ms vibration sensor, temperature is the worst under FP (76.7% on time, 6.8% missed). Vibration is close behind (79.9% on time, 9.8% missed) and is the only stream under FP that missed any alerts, 0.33 of 11 true events per run. Air quality (500 ms) stays best at 85%, and almost nothing is missed.

**Why?** A long read of 40-160 ms blocks the whole loop for one to three periods of a fast stream, but for less than one period of a slow one. The sensors with the shortest periods have the least slack, so they are hit hardest.

**Adding the fourth stream hurt everyone, although the gateway was only busy 47% of the time.** Average load looks safe, but the loop cannot be interrupted, so the tail of the read time decides. On-time temperature samples fell from 94.0% to 76.7%.

**FP against EDF:** the two are almost identical (76.7% against 76.6% for temperature). Here every deadline equals the next release, so the stream with the shortest period usually also has the earliest deadline. They would separate only if deadlines were different from periods. **Round-robin** is worse for the fastest stream: it cuts vibration to 65.0% on time and 16.3% missed, and with 3 streams it cuts temperature to 79.5%. It looks better for the slow streams (air quality 93.1% against 85.1% with 4 streams), but the gain is paid for by the fast ones.

**Alert delay** is not mostly a scheduling effect. Air quality and humidity alerts arrive on average about 170-220 ms after the true crossing, because they are sampled only every 240-500 ms. Vibration alerts arrive after about 30 ms, but its short bursts are the ones most likely to fall between samples.

**Recommendation:** put the shortest period first (FP or EDF), and shorten the worst-case read time. Cutting the 160 ms tail is likely to help more than changing the rule (this was not tested separately).

## 5. Limits of the model

The signals, long-tail read times and the 20% on-time tolerance are assumptions. The gateway has one core and no pre-emption. Dropping stale samples is a design choice; a system that queued them would show different numbers.
