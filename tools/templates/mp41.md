# Mini Project 4.1: Sampling Jitter and Processing Overruns

## 1. What this project does

A sensor loop should read the signal every 50 ms (20 Hz). Real embedded systems cannot keep that promise perfectly. This project simulates two kinds of timing error and measures how much they hurt a simple controller.

- **Jitter:** a sample is taken a little early or late. Each cycle gets a random shift between -J and +J.
- **Overrun:** the computation in one cycle takes longer than the period (60-150 ms instead of 4-8 ms). The next cycle cannot start until it finishes.

The "real" signal is a sine wave (amplitude 1, period about 2.09 s) with a tiny continuous ripple. The controller fires when the signal rises through the threshold 0.5. The **ideal loop** samples exactly every 50 ms. The **realistic loop** adds jitter and overruns. Both see the same signal.

## 2. How the simulation works

- Each run has 400 cycles (20 s). Every setting is repeated with 30 random seeds, and tables show the average of the 30 runs.
- Cycle k is planned at k x 50 ms plus its jitter. The loop is single-threaded, so the sample cannot happen before the previous cycle has finished. The command is issued when the cycle's computation ends.
- After an overrun, the default policy is **rebase** (like a PLC scan: the next scan starts when the last one ends, so the schedule slips later for good). A second policy, **catch-up**, keeps the original schedule and runs late cycles back-to-back until the loop is on time again.
- **Delay vs ideal** means: actuation time in the realistic loop minus actuation time in the ideal loop, for the same true threshold crossing. This is the lab's main metric. It can be negative because the ideal loop also has a built-in delay (its own sampling phase).
- **Latency after true crossing** means: actuation time minus the exact moment the true signal crossed the threshold. It is never negative.
- The **P-controller** (extra task) uses u = 2 x (0 - y). Its error is the RMS difference between its output and the ideal loop's output, as a percentage of the ideal output's RMS.

Sanity check: with no jitter and no overrun the realistic loop matches the ideal one (mean absolute delay about 1 ms, which is only the random computation time of 4-8 ms against a fixed 6 ms).

## Required summary

Overruns hurt more than jitter, and the difference is large. Compare the two at the same average disturbance of 5 ms per cycle (extra task 1, 30 runs). With jitter only (J = 10 ms), the mean latency after the true crossing is 29.9 ms and the worst case is 53.5 ms. With overruns only (p = 9.1%), the mean is 46.6 ms and the worst case is 121.2 ms. The P-controller output error is 4.8% for jitter and 12.5% for overruns. In the main sweep, 30% jitter (standard deviation 12 ms) has a worst-case latency of 60 ms, while a 5% overrun rate has 109 ms.

There are two reasons. First, jitter is symmetric. A sample that is early in one cycle and late in the next averages out, so the loop stays close to its schedule. An overrun only pushes in one direction: it delays the next sample and never speeds anything up. Second, with the PLC-style policy the delay accumulates. After 20 s, jitter-only runs have 0 ms of schedule lag, but runs with 5% overruns are on average 1.1 s behind the original schedule. The catch-up policy removes this lag, but it does not remove the damage: at 5% overruns its worst-case latency is still about 109 ms and its mean latency is slightly higher (42.9 ms against 39.1 ms).

Jitter is not harmless. At 50% of the period it already makes 12.6% of cycles longer than 1.5 periods, and about 0.4 threshold events per run are handled a full cycle late. But a bounded jitter can only delay an action by about one period, while one overrun can delay it by two or three periods.

The standard deviation of the sampling interval can mislead. Jitter at 30% of T has a larger standard deviation (12.2 ms) than 2% overruns (8.2 ms), yet the overruns cause more cycles beyond 1.5 T (1.7% against 1.3%) and a larger worst-case latency (63 ms against 60 ms). A compliance measure such as the percentage of cycles over 1.5 T is closer to what the controller feels. Designers should therefore remove overruns first: shorten the worst-case computation time, or watch the execution time and drop work.

## 6. Limits of the model

The signal, jitter distribution (uniform) and overrun durations (uniform 60-150 ms) are assumptions, not measurements of a real device. The loop is single-threaded with no pre-emption. The conclusions about which hazard is worse depend on the overrun length being longer than one period, which is the lab's definition.
